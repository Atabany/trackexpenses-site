How it is calculated

Yearly cost = price × times × periods in a year. A year counts as 365 days, 52 weeks or 12 months, depending on how you entered the habit. The month is the yearly cost divided by 12. The week is seven days of a daily habit, and the yearly cost divided by 52 for one counted by the week or the month. In the example, 4.50 five times a week is 22.50 a week; 52 weeks make 1,170 a year, which is 97.50 a month.

The five- and ten-year figures assume the price never changes. Prices usually rise, so read them as the least the habit would cost. The line about buying one fewer is the price times the periods in a year: at 4.50, skipping one coffee a week is 4.50 × 52, or 234 a year.

What the number is for

The yearly figure is not a verdict. A daily coffee that costs 1,170 a year may be worth every bit of it to you; the point is to decide with the real number in front of you, because a price seen once at the counter never looks like much. Two comparisons make it useful:

  • Against your pay. Enter your monthly take-home pay and the result shows the habit's share of it.
  • Against your fixed costs. A habit of 100 a month weighs the same as a 100 a month subscription. If you are looking for room in a budget, compare both lists before choosing what to change.

Find how often you really buy it

People usually guess the price right and the count wrong. Before trusting the result, count: open your card or bank statement for last month and count the purchases at that shop, or search your expense records for its name. If you pay in cash, keep the receipts for two weeks and double the count.

See it in your own spending

TrackExpenses can do the counting once purchases are in your ledger. Search lists every entry for a shop or a word, so you can count them. Questions

How much does a daily coffee cost in a year?

Multiply the price by 365. A coffee at 4 every day is 1,460 a year; bought only on five days a week it is 1,040 a year.

How do I work out the yearly cost of a habit?

Multiply the price by how many times you buy it in a week, then by 52. For something bought monthly, multiply the monthly spending by 12.

Do small purchases matter more than big bills?

Not necessarily. Rent, loans and subscriptions are often larger in total. Work out the yearly cost of each and compare them before deciding what to change.
