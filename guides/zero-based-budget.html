Zero-based budget: give every unit of pay a job

By Mohamed Elatabany · Updated November 2026 · Suggest a correction

A zero-based budget plans your whole take-home pay before the month starts, so income minus everything planned, bills, spending and savings, comes to exactly zero. Zero doesn't mean an empty account; it means no part of your pay is left without a job.

The method in four steps

Work with one pay period and the money that actually reaches you after tax and deductions.

  • Start with take-home pay. Say 12,000 a month.
  • Plan the fixed bills. Rent 4,300, utilities 450, phone 150 and insurance 300: 5,200.
  • Plan savings as a line, not a leftover. 1,500 to a savings account, decided now rather than hoped for at the end of the month.
  • Plan everyday spending by category. Groceries 1,800, transport 700, eating out 900 and personal 600: 4,000.

Planned so far: 5,200 + 1,500 + 4,000 = 10,700, which leaves 1,300 without a job. Give it one: 800 for irregular costs, such as a car service or a gift, and 500 more to savings. Now 12,000 − 12,000 = 0, and the budget is zero-based.

If the plan comes out below zero, you have planned money you don't have: lower a category before the month starts, not after the account runs dry.

Do the arithmetic on iPhone

Calculator on iPhone handles one month: 12,000 − 5,200 − 1,500 − 4,000 = 1,300 tells you how much still needs a job.

For a plan you reuse, Numbers is better. Put each planned amount in its own row and add a cell for income minus the SUM of the planned column. When that cell reads 0, every unit has a job. Apple's Numbers formula guide shows how to build it on the phone.

Zero-based and 50/30/20

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay by share: half for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings. Zero-based budgeting is stricter about the detail: every unit has a named line, whatever the shares turn out to be. They work well together. Use the 50/30/20 calculator to set rough targets, then fill in the lines until nothing is left to plan. The 50/30/20 guide has a worked example.

Plan it in TrackExpenses with the salary plan

The salary plan, part of Pro, is a zero-based budget on one screen. Open Stats → Budget and tap Salary plan at the top, or open it from More → Budgets.

  • Add your income. Tap Add your income and enter the Monthly income you expect: salary and any other regular income.
  • Spending. Each category's line is that category's budget, and Everything else is the part of your budget total no category holds, so nothing you spend falls outside the plan.
  • Saving & investing. Each Savings or Investment account gets a line for what you plan to put into it. Add such an account in Accounts first.

Above the lines, Planned and Left to plan add it up, and a sentence under the income answers the question the plan exists for. "Your income covers your plan exactly." is a zero-based budget. "Your income covers your plan, with 1,300.00 still to plan." means some money has no job yet; "Your income is 200.00 short of your plan." means you have planned more than you earn.

Editors open empty, with the current amount shown as a hint, and suggest figures you can take in one tap: your repeating bills, or what you spent or saved last period. Before you save, each one says what else moves, such as "Everything else becomes 1,900.00". You can type sums too: 1200 + 350 in an amount is saved as 1,550.

Switch from Plan to Actual during the month to see what was Received, Spent and Saved, and how much of what arrived is left after spending and saving. The plan is the budget, not a second one: the category lines are the same budgets the pacing bars, budget alerts and daily budget use. To keep to the plan during the month, the envelope budgeting guide shows how to move money between categories.

Months that aren't like the others

Extra income. A bonus or a side payment belongs to one month only. Tap Add extra income and enter it; your usual income stays as it is, and next month starts without it. While the extra is unplanned, every line's editor offers Extra income not yet planned, so giving the bonus a job is one tap, and changes made in that month apply to that month alone.

Logged income. Income you record never changes the plan by itself, because it may be next month's salary paid early. When more arrives than you planned, the plan says how much and leaves the decision to you.

A different month ahead. A change carries to the months after it unless a month has its own amount, and months already lived keep what they had. A note on a month, such as "Eid expenses" or "Holiday gifts", explains why December looks different.

The budget planner page shows the screens.

What it doesn't do

The salary plan is part of Pro, including Lifetime. Budgets themselves, category budgets, the bars and moving money between budgets are free, so you can run a zero-based plan by hand with a free budget: set the total to your take-home pay and give it out across categories, leaving Everything else for what is left.

Savings lines exist only for Savings or Investment accounts, and the free ledger holds three accounts. A line is a plan; the app doesn't move money for you. Record the transfer to savings, and Actual shows what reached the account.

The plan doesn't group lines into needs and wants. Its period is your budget month with a fixed start day, so pay every two weeks needs a monthly convention you keep.

Frequently asked questions

Does a zero-based budget mean spending everything I earn? No. Savings and irregular costs are lines like any other. Zero means every part of your pay is assigned, not that it is spent.

What if my income changes from month to month? Plan on the lowest income you can count on. When a better month comes, add the difference as extra income for that month and give it a job then.

Is the salary plan free? It is part of Pro, including Lifetime. Budgets, category budgets and moves between them are free.

How is zero-based different from envelope budgeting? Zero-based decides where every unit of pay goes before the month; envelopes are how you keep to it during the month, moving money between categories when one runs short. The envelope budgeting guide covers that part.
