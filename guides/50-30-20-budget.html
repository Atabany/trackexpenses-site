50/30/20 Budget: How to Split Your Take-Home Pay

By Mohamed Elatabany · Updated November 2026 · Suggest a correction

To do a 50/30/20 budget, take your monthly take-home pay and plan 50% of it for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings: on a take-home of 12,000 that is 6,000, 3,600 and 2,400. Then sort every spending category into one of the three groups and check, each month, that your actual spending stayed close to those amounts.

What counts as needs, wants and savings

The split was popularised by the book All Your Worth by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi (2005). It works on take-home pay: what reaches your account after tax and deductions. Where salaries are not taxed, that is simply the salary that arrives.

  • Needs (50%) are payments you would have to make even in a tight month: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, getting to work, childcare and the minimum payment on any loan or card.
  • Wants (30%) are the things you choose: eating out, delivery, shopping, entertainment, travel and most subscriptions.
  • Savings (20%) is money you keep: an emergency fund, investments, retirement, and loan payments above the minimum.

Decide the grey areas once and keep them there. A gym membership can be a want; a phone plan is usually a need, an upgrade is a want. Consistency matters more than the perfect label: the point is to compare months.

Work out your three amounts on iPhone

The 50/30/20 budget calculator does the arithmetic in your browser. On iPhone, open Calculator, type your take-home pay and multiply by 0.5, then 0.3, then 0.2. Apple's Calculator guide covers the basic keypad.

For a reusable sheet, open Numbers, type your take-home pay into one cell and add three result cells. Apple's Numbers formula instructions explain how to tap a result cell, open the Formula Editor, tap the pay cell and multiply it by 0.5, 0.3 or 0.2. When your pay changes, the three amounts update with it.

Then list your categories in two columns, needs and wants, and write a monthly amount beside each. Add the needs: if they come to more than half your pay, you have found the real constraint before you spend anything.

A worked example with real numbers

Take-home pay is 12,000 a month. The targets are 6,000 for needs, 3,600 for wants and 2,400 for savings.

Here is one way to fill the three groups, with each line's share of pay:

  • Needs: Rent 4,000 (33%), Groceries 1,200 (10%), Transport 500 (4%), Utilities and phone 300 (3%). Total 6,000, 50%.
  • Wants: Dining out 1,000 (8%), Shopping 800 (7%), Entertainment 600 (5%), Everything else 1,200 (10%). Total 3,600, 30%.
  • Savings: transfer to a savings account 2,400. Total 2,400, 20%.

Planned spending is 9,600, which is 80% of pay, and with savings the plan uses all 12,000. The shares are rounded to whole numbers, so a group's lines can add up to one percent more or less than its total.

If rent were 5,000 instead, needs would reach 7,000, or 58%. The honest choices are to accept a different split for now, such as 60/20/20, or to cut a need. Taking the difference out of savings without noticing is how a 50/30/20 plan quietly stops working.

Plan it in TrackExpenses

TrackExpenses does the same split with its salary plan, a Pro feature. Open Stats, choose Budget and tap Salary plan, the first row.

  • Income: tap Add your income and enter your take-home pay under Monthly income. If a repeating salary rule exists, the Repeating income suggestion fills it in with one tap.
  • Spending: under Spending, tap each need and want and enter its amount. The editor reminds you that this is the category's budget too, so the plan and your budget bars always agree. Categories you do not plan one by one are counted in Everything else.
  • Savings: add a Savings or Investment account in the Accounts tab, if you have none, then plan your 20% on it under Saving & investing.

Every line shows its share of your income, and the Spending and Saving & investing headers show their totals with a percentage. In the example, Spending reads 9,600 and 80%, Saving & investing 2,400 and 20%, and the plan says Your income covers your plan exactly. Plan less and it tells you how much is still to plan; plan more and it says how much your income is short.

Your plan follows your budget month, so if you are paid on the 25th, set More → Period & Entry first; the payday budget guide walks through it. Since version 1.10.0, each month can keep its own amounts: a change carries to later months unless one has its own, and months already lived keep what they had.

Check it at the end of the month

The budget only helps if you compare it with what happened. Switch the salary plan from Plan to Actual to see Received, Spent and Saved for the month, and on each line how much you spent of the planned amount and how much is left or over. Saved is what reached the account: money moved or paid in, less anything taken out.

During the month, the budget pacing guide explains how to read the spending bar against the today marker. If one category runs over while another has room, Move money between them for this month only; see the budget planner page.

Record a transfer to savings as a transfer, not an expense, or it will be counted twice. The credit card guide explains the same rule for card payments.

Limits to know

The app does not label categories as needs or wants. You decide which category belongs where and add up each group from its lines; naming categories clearly helps.

Saving & investing only lists Savings and Investment accounts. Extra loan payments are not a line there, so plan them with your own category if you record them as spending.

The salary plan requires Pro. Without it, you can still set a total and category budgets under More → Budgets and record transfers to a savings account, but the plan's income, percentages and Plan versus Actual view are Pro. The free version includes 3 accounts. Everything depends on your records: an unrecorded cash purchase is invisible to any budget. This guide explains a method, not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is 50/30/20 based on gross or take-home pay? Take-home pay: what you actually receive after tax and deductions. Using gross pay overstates every amount.

What if my needs are more than 50%? Write down the real figure and adjust the split, for example 60/20/20, while you work on the largest need. A plan you can follow is more useful than a ratio you always miss.

Do loan payments count as needs or savings? The minimum payment is a need. Anything above the minimum is part of the 20% savings group.

Can TrackExpenses show my 50/30/20 percentages? The Pro salary plan shows each line's share of your income and the total share for spending and for saving. It does not group categories into needs and wants for you.
